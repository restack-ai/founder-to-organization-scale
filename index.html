The bottleneck · diagnosis
The problem is undifferentiated centralization

Some decisions should stay with the founder — mission, capital, existential bets. The failure is being the default approver for everything else too. Three costs compound:

cost 1
Decision latency
Work queues behind one person. Throughput is capped by one calendar, not by the team.
cost 2
Context compression
Local knowledge must be translated upward before action — and loses fidelity on the way.
cost 3
Ownership erosion
People learn that deciding is risky and asking is safe. Senior talent leaves or stops acting senior.
the bottleneck · diagnosis
core thesis
Companies do not scale by making the founder faster.

They scale by moving sound decisions closer to the people — and systems — with the best local information, while preserving coherence through explicit principles, decision rights, metrics, and reviews.

The operating
system
Ownership is not announced — it is engineered. Decision rights, named owners, written context, escalation rules.
Operating system · the shift
A person-based system vs a scalable one
founder-driven
  • Founder is the de facto approver for most decisions.
  • Fast at small scale, then queue depth wins.
  • Context stays oral and personality-dependent.
  • Attracts executors; fragile to founder absence.
ownership-driven
  • Decision rights pushed to named DRIs inside guardrails.
  • More setup, then parallel decisions raise total throughput.
  • Context is written — decisions move without hierarchy.
  • Attracts owners; resilient to growth and absence.
The founder does not disappear. The founder moves from default decision-maker to designer of the decision system.

None of this applies all at once. Adopting structure too early is a failure mode too — match the system to the coordination load.

Your stage · failure modes
Delegation fails quietly, in both directions
  • DRI in name only — an owner is announced, but decisions still close in founder 1:1s.
  • Boomerang delegation — the first imperfect outcome quietly returns the decision to the founder.
  • Hidden approval — "keep me in the loop" behaves like a veto.
  • Crisis re-centralization that never reverts — the takeover was right; the missing expiry date wasn't.
the opposite failure is real too
Process heavier than the problem.

Zappos replaced managers with holacracy — explicit roles, maximal process. Adoption stalled, an opt-in-or-leave ultimatum followed, and roughly 18% of the company took severance. By 2020 it had quietly backed away. The fix for a founder queue is the minimum explicit structure, not the maximum.

Every failure has the same fix at its core: make the implicit explicit, in writing, before it repeats.

04
The first
90 days
The playbook for the messy middle — one quarter, two or three decision families, measured honestly.
First 90 days · playbook
Instrument, transfer, hold the line, review
weeks 1–2
Instrument
Log every decision the founder touches. Baseline the KPIs. Change nothing, announce nothing.
weeks 3–4
Transfer
Sort habit from requirement. Move 2–3 decision families with written delegation contracts — not ten.
weeks 5–8
Hold the line
Redirect asks to the owner, in public, every time. The first imperfect decision is tuition, not evidence.
weeks 9–12
Review & expand
Quarterly review against the baseline. Fix the contract, not the person. Move the next families down.
the real test
At least one delegated decision the founder disagreed with was allowed to stand. A system the founder can override at will is still a founder queue with extra steps.

05
The AI-agent era
Agents change the shape of organizational scale — they redistribute how decisions are prepared, routed, and executed.
AI-agent era · the paradox
Preparation decentralizes. Governance centralizes.
what decentralizes
  • Context gathering — agents summarize, synthesize, inspect metrics.
  • Decision drafting — memos, options, implementation plans.
  • Routing — work finds the right owner without a meeting.
  • The cost of preparing a good decision drops for everyone.
what centralizes
  • Tool and data-class policy — what agents may touch.
  • Approval thresholds — what requires a human.
  • Evals and observability — logs, traces, rework rates.
  • Accountability — every agent action maps to a human owner.
In poorly designed agent companies, people stop waiting for the founder and start waiting for unclear policy. The founder bottleneck becomes a policy bottleneck — govern early, lightly, in writing.
ai-agent era · the paradox
AI-agent era · autonomy tiersprepare broadly · execute narrowly
Delegate to agents like you delegate to people: by risk
tierexample workagent autonomyhuman role
LowDrafts, summaries, ticket classificationfull — draft and routeReviews when useful
ModerateInternal reversible actions, bounded workflowsinside policy + approvalsSpot-checks and audits
HighCustomer-facing changes, production, moneyprepare onlyApproves before execution
CriticalHiring, compensation, legal, security exceptionssupport onlyDecides — always
An agent can be assigned a task. It cannot be assigned moral, legal, or executive accountability. Raise autonomy only when evals are stable, logs are inspectable, and rollback exists — never because the demo looked good.
